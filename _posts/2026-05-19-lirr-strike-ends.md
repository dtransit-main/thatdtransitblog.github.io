On Saturday, May 16th at midnight, workers belonging to five unions walked off of their jobs at the Long Island Rail Road, suspending service for the foreseeable future as part of a planned workers' strike. A total of 3,500 LIRR workers from the Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers and Trainmen (BLET), Brotherhood of Railroad Signalmen (BRS), International Association of Machinists and Aerospace Workers (IAMAW), International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers (IBEW), and Transportation Communications Union (TCU) began their strike after negotiations between the unions, collectively known as the 'LIRR Bargaining Board', and the MTA did not find a resolution. 

After 3 full days of suspended service, New York Governor Kathy Hochul announced an agreement had been met between the two sides, and LIRR service would gradually resume beginning at noon on Tuesday, May 19th. Here's a deep dive into how the strike unfolded, and why it happened in the first place.


## Long Island Rail Road and its Unions

We'll start with the basics. The Long Island Rail Road, or LIRR, is America's busiest commuter rail network, shuttling over 330,000 people every single weekday between four western New York City terminals and the majority of Long Island. Owned and operated by the state-run MTA, LIRR operates 11 lines over 126 stations and 700 miles of trackage. With over 100 million riders per year, it surpasses the ridership numbers of sister operation Metro-North Railroad and New Jersey Transit to claim top spot in the United States of America.

Like every railroad operation in the United States, a majority of the LIRR workforce is represented by workers' unions. 3,500 of them are represented by the five listed above, who help defend the workers' rights to fair wages, consistent increases and fair treatment from the MTA. Every few years, the MTA and the unions will renegotiate a contract that ensures fair payment to the workers and consistent raises for the next few years. 

## How the Strike Started

For the past three years, the unions had been working without a contract - that meant no pay raises since 2023, in a time where the cost of living in the New York metro accelerated upwards with tremendous speed. The unions and the MTA had consistently butted heads over two topics in particular:
1. **Yearly raises** - To offset the effects caused by the lack of a new contract in three years, the unions requested an increased wage raise for the fourth year, around 5% - while the MTA would not budge higher than 3%.
2. **Work-rule and overtime** - The MTA attempted to put into place operational reforms that would change what it called 'outdated and expensive work rules.' One example of this - if an engineer were to drive an electric train and a diesel train in the same day, by the union contract, the MTA is required to compensate the engineer with two days' worth of pay. 

The unions argued that they deserved the wage increase, largely due to the lack of contract for three years, as well as the heightening cost of living in the New York City area. The MTA, meanwhile, argued that such wage increases would be a large financial drain on the agency, forcing them to raise fares on riders by as much as 8%.

The Taylor Law prevents public-sector employees from striking in the state of New York - preventing systems like the New York City Subway from shutting down. But because the LIRR falls under the federal Railway Labor Act (RLA), they are allowed to strike only after a lengthy dispute-resolution process, which includes mediation, federal intervention and mandatory cooling-off periods. The unions involved had already voted to authorize a strike in 2025, gone through federal mediation with a Presidential Emergency Board that sided with the unions, and completed the required cooling-off period, which expired in May 2026 - making a strike legally permissible.

## The Strike
At 12:00 a.m. on Saturday, May 16th, having given the MTA adequate warning of their intention to walk off the job, 3,500 of LIRR's workforce belonging to the five unions walked off their jobs after running their services to the terminal station, suspending Long Island Rail Road service systemwide. While weekday commutes would be supplemented by free shuttle buses provided by the MTA, there was no replacement for service on the weekends - with the MTA recommending that riders find their own way to the easternmost New York City Subway stations in eastern Queens and Brooklyn. As expected, a lack of rail service put major stress on Long Island's cross-island highways, including the LIE (I-495), the Northern and Southern State Parkway and the Belt Parkway to name a few. Riders who traditionally spent 60-90 minutes traveling to the city were now spending 3-5 hours on their trips.

By Monday morning, the dispute continued, meaning that riders would have to deal with reaching their jobs without the Long Island Rail Road. MTA's shuttle buses ran from major LIRR stations, like Hicksville, Huntington and Ronkonkoma, but took just a fraction of the total people that would traditionally rely on public transit. Long lines, overcrowding, delayed departures, and long rides were just a portion of the complaints that riders reported, braving through a morning without the LIRR. The evening commute looked similar, and it did appear that riders would be braving through this ordeal indefinitely...

## The Resolution

But, as with any strike, the point is to show the public just how vital the work of these employees is to the general public. And the five unions who walked off the job at the LIRR proved that point - Long Island fell into disarray without their tried-and-true rail network, which affected hundreds of thousands of people's lives. 
But on Monday night, after intensified pressure from New York Governor Kathy Hochul, a deal was reached between state officials, MTA leadership, and union negotiators. While details of the deal weren't released, Governor Hochul communicated that the deal improved wage terms, preserved key worker protections, and compromises on operational reforms. 

_<center>"Tonight, the MTA reached a fair deal with the five LIRR unions that delivers raises for workers while protecting riders and taxpayers.
I’m pleased to announce that phased LIRR service will resume beginning tomorrow at noon."</center>_
<p class="caption"><small><em>Governor Hochul's communication on social media confirming a deal had been reached to end the strike.</em></small></p>

Following this agreement, the MTA confirmed that LIRR service would resume on Tuesday, May 19th at 12pm, following thorough inspections of the rail network to ensure that no infrastructure was damaged during the three-day dormant period of the LIRR. Gradual service is beginning on the Port Washington, Huntington, Ronkonkoma and Babylon branches, with increased service coming later in the day.

# What To Take From This!

A strike to any railroad can be debilitating to the local economy, but in the Northeast, it is particularly devastating. The wide-ranging impact of the strike is clear evidence of just how much the local population relies on public transit, and how a lack of it affects everyone - making roadways even more miserable, increasing air pollution, and affecting opportunity for both the big businesses in New York City, as well as the small businesses across Long Island. 

The strike served as a reminder that rail networks are not just a convenience provided to a particular subset of people, but a critical component of infrastructure that affects everyone. It also worked - as mentioned before, a strike serves to show the public how important certain public workers are to an economy and a society, and the five unions representing the LIRR workforce did exactly that.
